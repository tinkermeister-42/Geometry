1.1Points, Lines, Segments, and Angles

  • A point is an exact location. A line goes on forever both ways. A plane is a flat surface that goes on forever.
  • Segment {{seg AB}}: two endpoints. Ray {{ray AB}}: starts at A, goes on forever. Line {{line AB}}. Length AB is a number.
  • {{ray AB}} and {{ray BA}} are different rays. The first letter is the endpoint.
  • An angle has two sides and a vertex. Name it ∠ABC with the vertex in the middle. Write the measure as {{mang ABC}}.
  • Acute: less than 90°. Right: 90°. Obtuse: between 90° and 180°. Straight: 180°.
  • Trust what is told and what is marked: square marks, tick marks, arrows. Do not trust how a figure looks.
{{fig 1.1_angle_types|angle types|1.7in}}
Example: Clock hands at 4:00: 4 · 30° = 120°, obtuse.

1.2Segment and Angle Addition

  • Length on a number line: AB = |a − b|. Length is never negative.
  • Segment addition: if B is between A and C, then AB + BC = AC.
  • Congruent (≅) is for figures. = is for numbers. {{seg AB}} ≅ {{seg CD}} means AB = CD.
  • A midpoint makes two congruent segments. A segment bisector goes through the midpoint.
  • Angle addition: if D is inside ∠ABC, then {{mang ABD}} + {{mang DBC}} = {{mang ABC}}. An angle bisector makes two congruent angles.
  • Protractor: put one side on a 0. Read the scale that starts at that 0.
{{fig 1.2_seg_add|segment AB plus BC equals AC|1.7in}}
Example: AB = 2x + 3, BC = x − 1, AC = 17. 3x + 2 = 17, so x = 5, AB = 13, BC = 4.

1.3Complementary, Supplementary, and Vertical Angles

  • Complementary angles add to 90°. Supplementary angles add to 180°. They do not have to touch.
  • Complement of x°: (90 − x)°. Supplement of x°: (180 − x)°.
  • Adjacent angles share a vertex and a side and do not overlap. A linear pair is adjacent with outside sides that are opposite rays. A linear pair is supplementary.
  • Vertical angles are across from each other. They are equal.
  • To solve for x: name the pair, then write angle + angle = 90 (complementary), = 180 (supplementary or linear pair), or angle = angle (vertical).
{{fig 1.3_vertical|two crossing lines making four angles|1.7in}}
Example: Vertical angles (4x − 10)° and (2x + 30)°. 4x − 10 = 2x + 30, so x = 20 and each angle is 70°.

1.4Parallel Lines and Transversals

  • Parallel (∥): same plane, never meet. Perpendicular (⊥): right angles. Skew: not in one plane, never meet.
  • A transversal crosses two lines and makes 8 angles. Pairs: corresponding, alternate interior, alternate exterior, same-side interior.
  • If the lines are parallel: corresponding, alternate interior, and alternate exterior angles are equal. Same-side interior angles are supplementary.
  • The rules work backward. Equal corresponding or alternate angles, or same-side interior angles that add to 180°, show the lines are parallel.
  • On a grid, parallel lines have the same slope.
{{fig 1.4_eight|two parallel lines cut by a transversal, with eight numbered angles|1.7in}}
Example: ℓ ∥ m. Corresponding angles (4x + 6)° and (6x − 24)°. x = 15, each angle 66°.

1.5Angles in Triangles and Polygons

  • The angles of a triangle add to 180°.
  • By sides: scalene, isosceles (two equal), equilateral (three equal). By angles: acute, right, obtuse, equiangular. Base angles of an isosceles triangle are equal.
  • An exterior angle of a triangle equals the sum of the two remote interior angles.
  • A polygon with n sides: angle sum (n − 2) · 180°. Exterior angles of a convex polygon add to 360°.
  • Regular polygon: each exterior angle is {{f 360/n}} and each interior angle is 180 minus that.
{{fig 1.5_diagonals|a hexagon cut into 4 triangles|1.7in}}
Example: Regular pentagon: (5 − 2) · 180 = 540, and 540 ÷ 5 = 108°.
