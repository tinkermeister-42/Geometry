3.5.1One After Another

  • A sequence of transformations does one transformation after another.
  • A → A′→ A′′. Always do the moves in the {{a}}order{{/a}} given.
  • A combined rule shows the whole sequence in one rule.
EX 1. Do a sequence

Translate P(2, 5) by (x + 3, y − 2). Then reflect the image over the y-axis.

Step 1: P′ = (2 + 3, 5 − 2) = {{a}}(5, 3){{/a}}

Step 2: P′′ = (−5, 3), so P′′ = {{a}}(−5, 3){{/a}}

Combined rule: (x, y) → ({{a}}−x − 3{{/a}}, {{a}}y − 2{{/a}}). Check: (−2 − 3, 5 − 2) = (−5, 3).

3.5.2Does Order Matter?

  • Two translations in either order give the {{a}}same{{/a}} image.
  • A reflection and a translation, in a different order, often give a {{a}}different{{/a}} image.
  • One example that gives different images is a {{a}}counterexample{{/a}} to the claim "order never matters."
EX 3. A counterexample

Use P(2, 1). Do these two moves in both orders: reflect over the x-axis, and translate by (x + 4, y − 3).

Reflect first: (2, −1). Then translate: {{a}}(6, −4){{/a}}.

Translate first: (6, −2). Then reflect: {{a}}(6, 2){{/a}}.

The images are different. So order {{a}}matters{{/a}} here.

3.5.3Describing a Sequence

  • Look at the {{a}}orientation{{/a}}. If it reversed, the sequence needs a reflection.
  • Compare sizes. If the size changed, the sequence needs a {{a}}dilation{{/a}}.
  • Check your sequence with one or two {{a}}vertices{{/a}}.
EX 6. Describe a sequence
{{fig 3.5_find|triangle ABC and its image after two moves|2.6in}}

△ ABC with A(1, 1), B(4, 1), C(1, 3) maps to △ A′′B′′C′′. Describe a sequence.

The labels go the other way around, so the sequence includes a {{a}}reflection{{/a}}.

Reflect over the y-axis: A′ = {{a}}(−1, 1){{/a}}. Then translate: A′′ = (−1, −4), which is {{a}}5 down{{/a}}: (x, y − 5).

Another answer: translate 5 down first, then reflect over the y-axis. It gives the same △ A′′B′′C′′.

3.5.4Rigid Motions and Dilations

TransformationKeeps lengths?Keeps angles?Rigid motion?
translationyesyesyes
reflectionyesyesyes
rotationyesyesyes
dilation (k ≠ 1){{a}}no{{/a}}{{a}}yes{{/a}}{{a}}no{{/a}}
  • Any sequence of rigid motions is a {{a}}rigid motion{{/a}}. It keeps lengths and angles.
  • A dilation changes {{a}}lengths{{/a}}. It keeps angles.

3.5.5Symmetry

  • Line symmetry: a {{a}}reflection{{/a}} over a line maps the figure onto itself. The line is a line of symmetry.
  • Rotational symmetry: a rotation of less than 360° maps the figure onto itself.
  • The order n is the number of times it fits in one full turn. The smallest angle is {{f 360°/n}}.
EX 10. Symmetry of letters
{{fig 3.5_letters|the letters H, S, and A|2.2in}}

Find the lines of symmetry and the order of rotational symmetry.

H: 2 lines (vertical and horizontal). Turn 180° and it fits. Order {{a}}2{{/a}}.

S: {{a}}0{{/a}} lines. Turn 180° and it fits. Order {{a}}2{{/a}}.

A: {{a}}1{{/a}} line (vertical). No turn less than 360° fits. Order {{a}}1{{/a}}.

EX 11. Regular polygons
{{fig 3.5_shapes|an equilateral triangle, a square, a regular pentagon, and a regular hexagon|3.0in}}

A regular polygon with n sides has n lines of symmetry and rotational symmetry of order n (see 1.5).

Equilateral triangle: {{a}}3{{/a}} lines, order {{a}}3{{/a}}, smallest angle {{f 360/3}} = {{a}}120°{{/a}}.

Square: {{a}}4{{/a}} lines, order {{a}}4{{/a}}, smallest angle {{f 360/4}} = {{a}}90°{{/a}}.

Regular pentagon: {{a}}5{{/a}} lines, order {{a}}5{{/a}}, smallest angle {{f 360/5}} = {{a}}72°{{/a}}.

A rectangle is not a square

Summary
dilationdoubleorderreflectionrigidsymmetry

1. After two moves, A becomes A′′, read "A {{a}}double{{/a}} prime".

2. Two moves in a different {{a}}order{{/a}} can give different images.

3. A sequence of rigid motions is a {{a}}rigid{{/a}} motion. A {{a}}dilation{{/a}} is not.

4. A regular polygon with n sides has {{a}}n{{/a}} lines of symmetry and its smallest rotation is {{f 360°/n}}.
