4.2.1Why Triangles? SSS

  • A triangle with three fixed side lengths can have only {{a}}one{{/a}} shape. A four-sided frame can tilt.
  • SSS (side, side, side): If three sides of one triangle are congruent to three sides of another triangle, the triangles are {{a}}congruent{{/a}}.
  • SSS is the rule behind the distance check on a grid in 4.1.

All three pairs of sides are congruent. By {{a}}SSS{{/a}}, △ ABC ≅ {{a}}△ YZX{{/a}}.

The letters follow the marks: A matches Y, B matches Z, and C matches X.

Triangles hold their shape
A bridge truss, a roof truss, and a bike frame are made of triangles. A triangle cannot be pushed out of shape. A rectangle can.

4.2.2SAS, ASA, AAS, and HL

  • SAS (side, angle, side): two sides and the angle {{a}}between{{/a}} them. The angle between two sides is the included angle.
  • ASA (angle, side, angle): two angles and the side {{a}}between{{/a}} them. The side between two angles is the included side.
  • AAS (angle, angle, side): two angles and a side that is {{a}}not between{{/a}} them.
  • HL (hypotenuse, leg): works for {{a}}right{{/a}} triangles only. The hypotenuse and one leg are congruent.

4.2.3What Is Not Enough: SSA and AAA

  • SSA (two sides and an angle that is not between them) is {{a}}not{{/a}} enough. Two different triangles can have these parts.
  • AAA (three angles) is {{a}}not{{/a}} enough. The triangles have the same shape but can have different {{a}}sizes{{/a}}.
  • A counterexample is one example that shows a rule does not always work.
EX 7. A counterexample for SSA
{{fig 4.2_ssa|triangle ABC and the larger triangle ABD share two sides and an angle|2.4in}}

In the figure, AB = 5, BC = BD = 3.2, and ∠A = 30° in both triangles. Two sides and a non-included angle match.

But AC is about 2.3 and AD is about 6.3. The triangles are {{a}}not{{/a}} congruent.

So SSA {{a}}does not{{/a}} show that triangles are congruent.

EX 8. A counterexample for AAA
{{fig 4.2_aaa|two triangles with the same angles and different sizes|2.4in}}

Both triangles have angles 40°, 60°, and 80°. The larger triangle has sides that are 2 times as long.

The angles match, but the sizes are different. The triangles are {{a}}not{{/a}} congruent. AAA {{a}}does not{{/a}} work.

Not enough does not mean not congruent
If the marks give only SSA or AAA, the triangles might be congruent. You just cannot tell from those facts. Write “not enough information”.

4.2.4Which Shortcut? Use the Marks and the Given Facts

  • Use only the marks and the facts you are given. A picture can {{a}}look{{/a}} congruent and not be.
  • A side that both triangles share is congruent to itself. It counts as one pair of {{a}}sides{{/a}}.
  • Vertical angles are {{a}}equal{{/a}} (1.3). Parallel lines make equal alternate interior angles (1.4).
  • Write the congruence statement with the letters in matching order. If no rule fits, write “not enough information”.
EX 10. A shared side
{{fig 4.2_shared|a kite with a diagonal|2.1in}}

AB ≅ AD and BC ≅ DC. Are △ ABC and △ ADC congruent?

Both triangles share {{seg AC}}, so AC ≅ AC.

Three pairs of sides are congruent. By {{a}}SSS{{/a}}, △ ABC ≅ {{a}}△ ADC{{/a}}.

{{fig 4.2_vert|two segments crossing at E|2.1in}}

{{seg AD}} and {{seg BC}} cross at E. AE ≅ DE and BE ≅ CE. Which rule shows △ AEB ≅ △ DEC?

{{ang AEB}} and {{ang DEC}} are vertical angles, so they are {{a}}equal{{/a}}.

EX 12. Parallel lines
{{fig 4.2_par|a parallelogram with a diagonal|2.3in}}

{{seg AB}} ∥ {{seg DC}} and AB ≅ DC. Which rule shows △ ABD ≅ △ CDB?

{{seg BD}} is a shared side. Because {{seg AB}} ∥ {{seg DC}}, the alternate interior angles {{ang ABD}} and {{ang CDB}} are {{a}}equal{{/a}}.

Two sides and the included angle match. By {{a}}SAS{{/a}}, △ ABD ≅ {{a}}△ CDB{{/a}}.

Angle A is not between {{seg AB}} and {{seg BC}}. This is SSA.

4.2.5Solve for x in Congruent Triangles

  • First use the marks to decide that the triangles are {{a}}congruent{{/a}}.
  • Then matching parts are congruent. Set them {{a}}equal{{/a}} and solve.
EX 15. Find x, y, and z
{{fig 4.2_solve|two triangles with algebra labels|2.8in}}

AC ≅ DF, BC ≅ EF, and ∠C ≅ ∠F. Find x, y, z, and AB.

Two sides and the included angle match. By {{a}}SAS{{/a}}, △ ABC ≅ △ DEF.

BC = EF, so 2y + 3 = 15 and y = {{a}}6{{/a}}.

∠C ≅ ∠F, so 5z + 1 = 61 and z = {{a}}12{{/a}}.

{{seg AB}} matches {{seg DE}}, so 3x + 1 = 5x − 9. Then 10 = 2x and x = {{a}}5{{/a}}.

AB = 3(5) + 1 = {{a}}16{{/a}}

EX 16. △ ABC ≅ △ DEF by SSS. AC = 4x − 7, DF = 2x + 9, BC = y + 5, and EF = 3y − 7. Find x, y, and BC.your turn
{{a}}

4x − 7 = 2x + 9, so x = 8. y + 5 = 3y − 7, so y = 6. BC = 11.

Summary
betweencongruentcounterexamplehypotenusenot enoughright

1. Two triangles are {{a}}congruent{{/a}} if SSS, SAS, ASA, AAS, or HL is true.

2. SAS needs the angle {{a}}between{{/a}} the two sides. ASA needs the side between the two angles. HL needs a {{a}}right{{/a}} triangle: {{a}}hypotenuse{{/a}} and a leg.

3. SSA and AAA are {{a}}not enough{{/a}}. One {{a}}counterexample{{/a}} shows a rule does not work.

4. Use only marks and given facts. A shared side, vertical angles, and parallel lines can give extra pairs.
